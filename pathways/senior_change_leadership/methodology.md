# Senior Change Leadership Methodology

## Source

North Star Proficiency Builder pathway definition for the September 2026 Senior Change Leadership real-world field experiment.

## Core Principle

This is a professional-development pathway, not a Change Management task-management pathway.

The pathway helps an experienced Change Management practitioner develop through structured reflection on real workplace experiences. The practitioner examines situations, decisions, behaviors, and outcomes; recognizes patterns in their own practice; deliberately experiments with alternative approaches; and builds evidence of professional growth over time.

## Development Dimensions

The pathway is organized around six developmental dimensions:

### 1. Strategic Change Thinking

Understand the larger organizational system, diagnose before acting, distinguish symptoms from underlying change issues, connect Change Management activity to business outcomes, recognize dependencies and organizational dynamics, and operate beyond deliverable execution.

### 2. Stakeholder Influence & Alignment

Understand stakeholder perspectives, recognize competing interests and concerns, build alignment, influence without formal authority, navigate resistance, recognize stakeholder dynamics, and adapt approaches based on stakeholder needs.

### 3. Executive Communication

Frame complex issues clearly, communicate at the appropriate level, adapt messaging to audience, make concise recommendations, communicate uncertainty appropriately, support decision-making, and demonstrate executive presence.

### 4. Facilitation & Leadership Presence

Lead productive conversations, ask effective questions, create clarity from ambiguity, facilitate alignment, manage tension constructively, listen before solving, and help groups move toward decisions.

### 5. Applied Change Judgment

Select interventions appropriate to the situation, apply methodology pragmatically rather than mechanically, recognize when additional diagnosis is required, balance strategy with execution, adapt Change Management practices to organizational context, and make sound professional judgments when the path is unclear.

### 6. Reflective Practice

Examine one's own behavior, question assumptions, consider alternative approaches, learn from outcomes, recognize recurring patterns, deliberately experiment with new behaviors, and incorporate learning into future practice.

## Stage Sequence

### Stage 1 — Establish My Practice

Establish the practitioner's professional baseline. Explore current role, perceived strengths, development objectives, Change Management philosophy, challenging situations, and an initial self-reflection across the six development dimensions.

### Stage 2 — Observe My Practice

Examine real workplace situations using the sequence: Situation → interpretation → approach → behavior → outcome → reflection. Treat one occurrence as an observation; repeated evidence may become a pattern.

### Stage 3 — Recognize Patterns

Connect observations across situations, distinguish isolated events from recurring behaviors, identify potential strengths and development opportunities, and validate interpretations with the practitioner.

### Stage 4 — Deliberate Practice

Convert meaningful developmental observations into small, intentional workplace experiments. Apply the experiment during real work and establish a practical development commitment.

### Stage 5 — Evaluate Outcomes

Revisit deliberate-practice experiments, describe what happened, examine stakeholder or situational response, identify what the practitioner learned, and decide whether to retain, adapt, or abandon the approach.

### Stage 6 — Demonstrate Growth

Review accumulated experiences, identify demonstrated development, recognize continuing development needs, update priorities, and establish the next area of deliberate practice. Use language of demonstrated growth, not permanent mastery.

## Privacy Guidance

The practitioner should describe workplace situations using anonymized roles rather than personally identifiable information. Examples: "Project Lead," "Senior Stakeholder," "Client Sponsor," "Workstream Owner," "Stakeholder A." Share only enough organizational context to support meaningful coaching.

## Coaching Orientation

The AI coach supplements a human coach and functions as a professional thinking partner. It does not replace the human advisor, does not deliver formal assessment, and does not award ratings, badges, or certifications.
